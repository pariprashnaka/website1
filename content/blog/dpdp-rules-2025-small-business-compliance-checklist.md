If your business keeps a customer list, a patient register, an employee file or a WhatsApp contact sheet, India's data protection law applies to you. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act was passed in August 2023, and the rules that make it enforceable were notified on 14 November 2025. The government is rolling them out in phases over roughly 12 to 18 months, so the full set of obligations lands around mid-2027.

That sounds distant. It is not. The expensive part of compliance is rarely the paperwork. It is discovering where personal data actually lives in your business, and that takes months.

## What the law actually says, in plain language

Under the Act, a business that decides why and how personal data is processed is a **data fiduciary**. The person the data belongs to is the **data principal**. A fiduciary must collect data with clear consent and notice, use it only for the stated purpose, keep it secure, and tell people and the regulator when something goes wrong.

The rules also set up a **Data Protection Board of India**, a fully digital body with four members. Complaints can be filed online, and appeals against its decisions go to the Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT). The rules keep what the government describes as a graded system, meant to avoid overwhelming smaller businesses.

## The penalties are the headline

The Act sets maximum penalties for a data fiduciary:

- **Up to ₹250 crore** for failing to maintain reasonable security safeguards
- **Up to ₹200 crore** each for failing to notify the Board and affected people of a breach, and for breaking the rules on children's data
- **Up to ₹50 crore** for any other violation

These are ceilings, not fines every business should expect. But they show where the regulator's attention is meant to go: **security and breach handling come first.**

## What this means for the kinds of businesses we work with

**A pathology lab** holds names, ages, phone numbers and health results. That is some of the most sensitive data a small business handles, and results often travel over WhatsApp and printouts. Children's data needs verifiable parental consent, though the rules exempt processing needed for essential services such as healthcare.

**A café or retailer** with a loyalty list or delivery addresses holds customer phone numbers and locations, usually in a shared spreadsheet that every staff member can open.

**Any employer** holds Aadhaar copies, bank details and attendance records for its staff.

In each case the weak point is the same: data scattered across spreadsheets, phones and chat apps, with no record of who accessed what.

## A practical checklist

1. **Map your data.** List every place personal data lives: software, spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups, paper registers, staff phones.
2. **Write a plain-language notice.** Say what you collect and why, at the point of collection.
3. **Record consent.** Know when and how each customer agreed. A ticked box in your system beats a verbal yes.
4. **Restrict access.** Give each staff member access only to what their job needs, and turn off shared logins.
5. **Keep an audit trail.** You cannot report a breach you cannot detect. Logs of who viewed or exported data matter.
6. **Plan for a breach.** Decide today who investigates, who informs the Board and affected people, and how.
7. **Handle requests.** People can ask to access, correct or erase their data, and requests must be answered within a maximum of 90 days. Know where each person's data sits so you can act.
8. **Review your vendors.** If a software vendor holds your customers' data, ask where it is stored and how it is protected.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does the DPDP Act apply to a small shop?**
It applies to any business that processes digital personal data. The rules keep a graded approach for smaller businesses, but the core duties around consent and security are not optional.

**When do I have to comply?**
The rules were notified on 14 November 2025 and are being phased in over 12 to 18 months. Some provisions took effect immediately and others, such as detailed notice and consent obligations, apply later in the window.

**What is the maximum penalty?**
Up to ₹250 crore for failing to maintain reasonable security safeguards. Other breaches carry lower ceilings, as shown above.

**Do I need parental consent for children's data?**
Yes. Verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian is required, with exemptions for processing tied to essential services such as healthcare, education or real-time safety.
